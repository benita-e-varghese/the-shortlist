# The Shortlist

## What this is for

People shopping for something real — a moisturiser, wireless earbuds, an air fryer, a carry-on bag — usually don't stall because they can't find options. They stall because there are too many options, too many reviews that contradict each other, and no way to tell which factor actually matters for them personally. This skill's job is to cut through that: ask what actually matters to *this* person, then make an actual call, not just hand back a comparison table and leave the decision on their desk.

Don't jump straight to recommending products the moment someone names a category. The value here is in the questions — they're what makes the eventual recommendation trustworthy instead of generic.

## Step 1: Ask 2–3 fit questions — nothing about budget yet

Ask exactly 2–3 short clarifying questions aimed at **fit and use-case**. Budget does not belong in this batch — it gets asked afterward, on its own, so it never quietly becomes the deciding factor just because it was easiest to ask first. If you notice yourself drafting more than 3 questions, cut it down to the 3 that would change the recommendation the most and drop the rest.

Some things to keep in mind:

- More than one thing can matter at once. Someone buying a moisturiser might have sensitive skin *and* dryness *and* occasional breakouts — all at the same time, not as alternatives. When a question has that shape, make clear they can give you more than one answer, and treat every answer they give as applying simultaneously.
- Ask about region only if it would change which brands or products are realistic to recommend — otherwise skip it.
- Keep questions short and concrete. "What's your skin like — dry, oily, sensitive, acne-prone, combination?" beats "Tell me about your skincare needs."

Wait for their answers before doing anything else.

## Step 2: Ask budget, as its own separate question

Once you have their fit answers, ask about budget by itself: something like "Any budget in mind? Totally fine to say 'no limit' — this is just one factor, not the deciding one." Don't bundle this into Step 1, and don't skip it even if they seem price-insensitive — let them opt out explicitly instead of assuming.

If they already volunteered a budget unprompted earlier, you can skip re-asking — just don't let it override the fit questions from Step 1.

## Step 3: Compare real options, weighing more than price

Recommend **4 to 6** realistic, currently-sold options appropriate for their market and their specific answers — not fewer. Weigh suitability, quality or build/ingredient considerations, durability, and reputation alongside price; price is one factor, not the sorting key. Say so explicitly if it's doing less work than usual in this particular recommendation.

For each option, give:

- **Name and typical price range** (a range, not a fabricated exact price — you don't have live pricing).
- **A one-line fit note** — why this does or doesn't suit *their* specific answers, not a generic blurb.
- **What's good about it, and what to be cautious about.** Real tradeoffs, not just upsides.

Then call one option **your pick**, and explain why in 2–3 sentences that tie directly back to what they told you — not "this is the best-reviewed" but "given you said X and Y, this fits because Z."

If you genuinely don't have enough signal to make a confident call between two options, say so rather than forcing a pick — but this should be rare once Steps 1–2 have actually been done.

## Step 4: Point them to where to verify it — never invent a specific link

This is the part most worth getting right, because getting it wrong is worse than saying nothing. You're working from general knowledge, not a live price feed — you do not know today's actual price, whether that exact listing still exists, or whether the product page you'd type from memory is even still the right URL. So:

- **Never** write out a specific product page URL, brand-site link, or "buy here" link for any option — not even one that looks plausible. A guessed link that happens to be wrong is worse than no link, because it reads as verified when it isn't.
- Instead, tell them what to type into a search: the exact product name, on a retailer appropriate to their region — e.g. "search Amazon.in for '[exact product name]'" for India, "search Amazon.com for '[exact product name]'" for the US, "search Amazon.co.uk" for the UK, or Google Shopping as a fallback. A search result they land on themselves is always current; a link you typed from memory might not be.
- This applies equally to "official" or brand-direct sites — you don't know if a brand renamed a product page, discontinued a line, or changed their URL structure since your knowledge was last updated.

Always close with one explicit line along these lines: *this is based on general product knowledge, not a live price feed — confirm the actual price, stock, and current model name before buying.* Don't fold this into a different sentence or drop it — it's the line that keeps the recommendation honest about its own limits.

## Output format

Write it conversationally, not as a rigid template — but always include, in this order: the fit questions (Step 1), the budget question (Step 2, once fit answers are in), the pick and the full set of options with their tradeoffs (Step 3), and the search-based verify links plus the caveat (Step 4). Bold option names and light use of headers is fine; don't over-format a short answer into a wall of nested bullets, and don't skip straight to a table of options before you've asked anything.

## Example

**User:** "Help me pick a pair of wireless earbuds, I'm in India, nothing too expensive."

**Response should:** ask 2–3 fit questions first (e.g. primary use — workouts / calls / commute noise / music quality — and whether noise cancellation matters), wait for answers, then ask budget on its own, then give 4–6 real options sold in India with one clear pick tied to their actual answers, search-style pointers (never guessed URLs), and the pricing/stock caveat as the last line.
